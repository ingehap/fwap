# Review of Full Waveform Acoustic Data Processing

**Mari, J.-L., Coppens, F., Gavin, Ph., and Wicquart, E. (1994).** *Full Waveform Acoustic Data Processing*. Translated by Gillian Harvey-Bletsas. Éditions Technip, Paris. 136 pp., paperback, 170 × 240 mm. ISBN 978-2-7108-0664-6. Originally published in French as *Traitement des diagraphies acoustiques*. Part of the Éditions Technip series on geophysics and signal processing by the IFP School / French Petroleum Institute authors .

This slim volume is one of the classic IFP-school references on what we would today call borehole acoustic signal processing. The authors' starting point is that compressional velocity logs have been routine since 1952, but digital recording of the full acoustic waveform now lets loggers apply the same signal-processing arsenal that is used in surface seismic, and that this richer processing yields a much deeper characterization of the formation . The book's strength is that it does not try to cover everything about sonic logging; instead it picks four concrete problems and works each one through, from raw multichannel waveform to a log curve a petrophysicist can actually hang next to a gamma-ray or a resistivity track.

The four parts are: (1) an artificial-intelligence approach for the picking of waves on full-waveform acoustic data, (2) wave separation in acoustic well logging, (3) reservoir characterization using acoustic logs obtained with the dipole sonic imaging tool, and (4) dip measurement based on acoustic data . This structure is both the book's virtue and its limitation. The virtue is that each chapter reads as a short, self-contained monograph with a genuine case study and IFP-developed algorithms behind it — notably the intercept-time (delay) method adapted to borehole data, which the same authors published in parallel as a journal paper (Coppens & Mari, 1995, *First Break* **13**(1), 11–20; and Coppens & Mari, 1995, *Revue de l'IFP* **50**(2), 143). The limitation is that the book is neither a tool catalogue nor a textbook on borehole wave physics: readers coming in cold will want a parallel reference such as Paillet & Cheng's *Acoustic Waves in Boreholes* or, from the same author team, *Signal Processing for Geologists and Geophysicists* (Mari, Glangeaud & Coppens, Éditions Technip, 1999) for the underlying wavefield theory and transforms.

The production is typical of the 1994 Éditions Technip series — compact, black-and-white log plots, clear equations, minimal narrative. Despite its age, the book holds up because the algorithms it codifies (semblance-style coherence picking, multichannel wave separation, dipole-flexural processing, intercept-time inversion for altered zones) are still the backbone of modern sonic processing chains, including those embedded in current commercial packages. For a working petrophysicist or processing geophysicist wanting a principled, worked-example view of full-waveform sonic processing, it remains a useful and surprisingly concise reference. For a novice, it is best read alongside a more general well-logging text such as Serra's *Well Logging Handbook*.

## Summary of the Main Ideas

### 1. Petrophysical Workflows

The unifying petrophysical idea of the book is that a sonic tool, once it records and digitizes the *entire* waveform at multiple receivers rather than just a first-arrival transit time, stops being a pure Δt tool and becomes a formation-characterization tool. The information obtained by acoustic data processing can be used for a more thorough characterization of formations , and each of the four parts of the book illustrates one such characterization workflow.

The first workflow is **automatic multi-wave log production**. Instead of delivering only a compressional slowness (DTc), the processing chain picks and tracks the compressional, shear and Stoneley wave trains along the whole logged interval, yielding continuous Vp, Vs and Stoneley slowness curves together with per-mode amplitude and coherence logs. These feed directly into classical petrophysical interpretation: Vp/Vs as a lithology and fluid-content indicator, shear-based elastic moduli (Young's modulus, Poisson's ratio, bulk and shear moduli) for geomechanics, and Stoneley attributes as proxies for fracture presence and permeability.

The second workflow is **near-wellbore integrity and altered-zone characterization**. By exploiting the fact that arrival times carry information not only about the bulk formation but about the source- and receiver-side paths, the authors show how the intercept-time (delay) method — a refraction-seismic idea — can be transposed to acoustic logs. Formation slowness is commonly estimated from first-motion detection of the compressional headwave, but additional information is embodied in the traveltimes themselves, because in the presence of near-borehole changes in formation slowness, such as altered or damaged zones implying a reduced velocity around the borehole, refracted arrivals may pass some inches to some feet from the borehole wall . The delivered logs — a "virgin" formation slowness plus delay logs at source and receiver depths — quantify the thickness and velocity contrast of the damaged/altered halo, which is of direct interest for mud-invasion diagnosis, cement-bond quality, wellbore stability, and correction of shallow-reading logs.

The third workflow is **dipole-sonic reservoir characterization**. Part 3 is explicitly reservoir characterization using acoustic logs obtained with the dipole sonic imaging tool . The dipole source lets the tool measure shear slowness even in slow (unconsolidated, gas-charged) formations where monopole tools fail, and the workflow extracts shear anisotropy, mechanical properties and fracture indicators from the flexural wave. This is the input track for sanding prediction, hydraulic-fracture design, stress-direction estimation and fracture-reservoir description.

The fourth workflow is **structural dip from acoustic data**. Rather than relying on a dedicated dipmeter, the book shows that the multi-receiver, azimuthally resolved acoustic record can itself be processed to yield dip and azimuth of bed boundaries and fractures intersecting the well, providing a secondary, independent structural log that can be cross-checked against the resistivity-based dipmeter or image log.

### 2. Computational Algorithms

The book's computational spine consists of four algorithm families, one per chapter.

**Artificial-intelligence-based wave picking.** Chapter 1 presents an artificial-intelligence approach for the picking of waves on full-waveform acoustic data . The problem is that a waveform at each receiver contains the P arrival, the S arrival, one or more pseudo-Rayleigh/guided modes, and the low-frequency Stoneley tube wave, all of which must be consistently identified over hundreds of depths despite noise, cycle skips and mode crossovers. Instead of a pure semblance or correlation scheme, the authors codify expert rules (expected slowness ranges, wavelet shape, onset polarity, coherence across the receiver array, depth-to-depth continuity) into a rule-based/expert-system picker that cooperates with a semblance-type coherence estimator. This is very much a 1990s-style "knowledge-based" processing pipeline and was one of the early and influential applications of symbolic AI in log processing.

**Wave separation on multichannel records.** Chapter 2 addresses the fact that P, S and Stoneley arrivals overlap in time but differ in apparent slowness across the receiver array. The algorithms are the multichannel, seismic-style velocity filters that the same team developed more fully in Mari & Glangeaud's *Wave Separation* (Éditions Technip). In practice this means working in the frequency–wavenumber (f–k) or τ–p domain, or with SVD/Karhunen–Loève–type projectors designed to isolate an apparent-slowness band. The output is a set of cleanly separated wave sections, one per mode, which both improves the AI picker upstream and allows mode-specific amplitude and dispersion analysis.

**Intercept-time (delay) inversion and dipole-flexural processing.** Chapter 3 combines two algorithmic ideas. First, the intercept-time adaptation developed by the authors in parallel work: a stand-alone method for the computation of the formation acoustic slowness and of the delay times from full-waveform acoustic data recorded with a multi-receiver tool, where the computation of the delay at each depth allows estimation of the extent of the well-altered zone . Algorithmically this is a least-squares refraction-style inversion applied in depth rather than in lateral offset, picking two constant-offset sections and decomposing the travel times into a formation-slowness component and per-depth source/receiver delays (Coppens & Mari, 1995, *First Break* **13**, 11–20). Second, the chapter covers dipole sonic imaging processing: because the flexural wave is dispersive and at low frequencies the shear and flexural waves have equal formation slowness values , the algorithms rely on narrow-band filtering plus slowness-time coherence (semblance) at low frequency, or explicit dispersion correction, to extract an unbiased shear slowness.

**Dip estimation from azimuthally distributed acoustic data.** Chapter 4 treats the acoustic array as a small borehole "antenna": a dipping reflector or bed crossing the well produces systematic, azimuth-dependent travel-time and amplitude variations across receivers. The algorithm is a cross-correlation / coherence search over candidate dip and azimuth values (a borehole analogue of seismic slant-stack or surface-wave beamforming), with the maximum of the coherence surface giving the dip angle and dip direction at each depth.

Cross-cutting all four chapters is a consistent processing philosophy: treat the borehole record as a small-scale seismic experiment, apply the corresponding seismic-processing toolbox (coherence/semblance, multichannel filtering, slowness inversion, dispersion analysis), and quality-control the result by log continuity and cross-consistency between modes.
