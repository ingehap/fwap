# Review: Tarantola (2005), Inverse Problem Theory and Methods for Model Parameter Estimation

## Context and contribution

*Inverse Problem Theory and Methods for Model Parameter Estimation* by Albert Tarantola was published by the Society for Industrial and Applied Mathematics (SIAM) in 2005 and runs 342 pages. It is the second and definitive version of the author's 1987 Elsevier volume *Inverse Problem Theory: Methods for Data Fitting and Model Parameter Estimation*, substantially rewritten and re-edited; SIAM makes the PDF freely available from its website, which together with the book's textbook-grade exposition has made it the most widely consulted single reference on inverse problems in geophysics. Tarantola, then at the Institut de Physique du Globe de Paris, had spent two decades developing the probabilistic, measure-theoretic view of geophysical inversion that this book consolidates; the formulation underlies essentially all of seismic tomography, full-waveform inversion, gravity and magnetic inversion, and reservoir-property estimation as practised since.

The book's enduring contribution is to insist on a *Bayesian, measure-theoretic* formulation of the inverse problem in which (i) the prior knowledge about the model is encoded as a probability density on the model space, (ii) the data and their uncertainties are encoded as a probability density on the data space, (iii) the forward physics is encoded as a conditional probability density relating model and data, and (iv) the solution of the inverse problem is the *posterior* probability density on the model space obtained by combining these three ingredients via the conjunction-of-states-of-information operation that Tarantola defines axiomatically. Deterministic least-squares inversion, regularized least-squares, MAP estimation, Tikhonov regularization, sparse-inversion variants, Monte Carlo and Markov-chain Monte Carlo all appear in this book as *specializations* of the underlying probabilistic formulation — typically obtained by assuming Gaussian or Laplacian distributions and then taking the maximum-a-posteriori point as the "solution".

A second contribution, often under-appreciated by users who treat the book only as a recipe for least squares, is Tarantola's careful insistence on the *metric* (volume element) of the parameter space. For parameters that take values on a positive half-line (densities, velocities, resistivities, conductivities), the appropriate prior is not flat in the parameter itself but flat in its logarithm (a Jeffreys prior). This single observation, that inverse-problem solutions depend on the way one parameterizes the model, is the source of much of the practical guidance in the book and remains the cleanest published treatment of the issue.

## Main ideas and structural progression

**Chapter 1 — The general discrete inverse problem.** The opening chapter axiomatises the entire framework. *States of information* on a manifold (model space or data space) are probability densities relative to a homogeneous (non-informative) reference measure. The book introduces the *conjunction* operator — pointwise product of two densities, normalized — as the formal way to combine two independent states of information about the same variable. Bayes' rule emerges as a special case of conjunction. The forward problem is presented as a conditional density θ(d|m), which in the limit of a deterministic forward operator g becomes a delta function δ(d − g(m)) but in general can carry "modelling uncertainty" reflecting an imperfectly known physics. The posterior on the model space is then σM(m) ∝ ρM(m) · ρD(g(m)) / μD(g(m)), where ρ are the priors, μ is the data-space reference measure, and g is the forward operator. This chapter is the most-cited part of the book; the *σ* notation for the posterior density is standard in subsequent geophysical-inversion literature.

A second sub-theme of Chapter 1 is the *resolution analysis*. For a linearized problem with Gaussian priors, the posterior covariance and the resolution operator R = (Cm⁻¹ + Gᵀ Cd⁻¹ G)⁻¹ Gᵀ Cd⁻¹ G are derived in closed form, and the chapter shows how to read trade-offs between resolution and posterior variance directly from R. The discussion of *checkerboard tests* and synthetic resolution tests as proxies for the formal resolution operator is the standard reference for those constructions.

**Chapter 2 — Monte Carlo methods.** When the posterior is non-Gaussian and the model dimension is moderate, the book argues that the right way to characterize σM is to *sample* it rather than to maximise it. The chapter develops rejection sampling, importance sampling, the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm, and the cascaded Metropolis sampler appropriate to geophysical problems where the forward map is the expensive ingredient. The treatment is careful about the relation between the proposal distribution, the prior, and the acceptance ratio, and is the place where many geophysicists learn MCMC. The chapter also makes the point — important in practice — that for highly nonlinear geophysical inverse problems the only honest output is a posterior *ensemble*, not a single best-fit model.

**Chapter 3 — The least-squares criterion.** The longest chapter. If the prior and likelihood are both Gaussian, the conjunction-of-states posterior is Gaussian and the MAP point coincides with the minimizer of the misfit functional

  S(m) = ½ (g(m) − dobs)ᵀ Cd⁻¹ (g(m) − dobs) + ½ (m − mprior)ᵀ Cm⁻¹ (m − mprior).

The chapter develops the optimization machinery for minimizing S: steepest descent, conjugate gradient, Gauss–Newton, full Newton, and the quasi-Newton (variable-metric, BFGS) methods that dominate in large-scale geophysical applications. The crucial subtlety of *preconditioning* — that the natural metric for the iteration is Cm, not the Euclidean metric — is developed with examples. For linearized full-waveform inversion this chapter is the standard reference for the construction of the gradient (the adjoint-state method, which Tarantola was among the first to apply to seismic-waveform inversion in his 1984 *Geophysics* paper), the Hessian, and the relation between Gauss–Newton and Newton steps. The chapter closes with the *covariance matrix of the estimate*, the posterior covariance about the MAP point, and emphasises that the formal posterior covariance is meaningful only to the extent that the Gaussian-and-linearized approximation is itself valid.

**Chapter 4 — Least-absolute-values criterion and minimax criterion.** Departures from Gaussianity. When the data errors are Laplacian rather than Gaussian, the maximum-likelihood estimator is the L1 fit rather than the L2 fit; when they are uniform on a bounded interval, the right estimator is a minimax / Chebyshev fit. The chapter develops the algorithms — iteratively-reweighted least squares for L1, linear programming for L∞ — and their statistical interpretation. This material is the foundation of the modern "robust" inversion literature and of compressive-sensing approaches to inversion that exploit Laplacian-equivalent sparsity priors.

**Chapter 5 — Functional inverse problems.** The discrete framework of the preceding chapters is lifted to continuous fields. Model "vectors" become functions on a continuous spatial or temporal domain, covariance matrices become covariance kernels, and the Gauss–Newton iteration becomes a flow on a function space whose tangent step is computed via the adjoint of a partial-differential operator. The chapter develops the adjoint-state method in this functional setting, which is the formal foundation of full-waveform inversion and of seismic tomography of the kind now routine in academic and industrial practice.

**Appendices.** A substantial set of appendices covers the mathematical infrastructure: probability theory, including the careful distinction between probability and "state of information"; vector and tensor algebra; the volume element / metric on a manifold and how it transforms under reparameterization; linear algebra (singular-value decomposition, generalised inverses); and convergence proofs for the iterative algorithms of Chapter 3. The appendices repay reading; the metric-on-the-manifold discussion is the cleanest statement in the literature of why the answer to a geophysical inverse problem can depend on whether one parameterizes the model in terms of velocity or slowness.

## Significance and limitations

The book's enduring value is that it is the single reference where every inversion technique used in geophysics is derived as a particular case of one underlying probabilistic formulation. Practising geophysicists routinely cite Tarantola (2005) for: the Bayesian setup of seismic inversion, the adjoint-state gradient of a least-squares misfit (the basis of FWI), the formal posterior covariance and resolution operator (the basis of tomographic resolution analysis), the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm in geophysical practice (the basis of stochastic inversion), and the choice of model-space metric (the basis of the log-parameterization that is standard for positive parameters). The book is also unusually careful about the *philosophy* of inversion, distinguishing genuine probability statements about reality from convenient computational fictions, in a way that has shaped how the field talks about its own results.

Three limitations are worth flagging. First, the book is fundamentally a *theory* and *algorithmics* monograph: with the partial exception of Chapter 5 it is not application-specific, and a reader who wants to do seismic-reservoir inversion will need to combine Tarantola's framework with the application-specific machinery in Grana, Mukerji & Doyen (2021) and Avseth, Mukerji & Mavko (2005). Second, the treatment of numerical inversion is largely *deterministic-optimization-with-Gaussian-priors* plus *Monte Carlo for non-Gaussian cases*; modern variational-Bayes, ensemble Kalman, and machine-learning-based inversion methods (variational autoencoders, normalising flows, score-based diffusion priors) are absent because they postdate the book. Third, the notation is occasionally idiosyncratic — the σ for posterior, the careful distinction between probability and homogeneous reference measure, the emphasis on tensor index notation in the appendices — and can be a stumbling block for readers coming from a mainstream statistics background. None of these qualifies the central claim that this is the single most influential book on inverse problems in geophysics in the past forty years.

For our project the book is the natural reference whenever the question is *what does it mean, in principle, to solve an inverse problem* — whether the inverse problem is rock-physics parameter estimation from logs, AVO inversion of seismic amplitudes, full-waveform inversion of seismic waveforms, or joint inversion of seismic and electromagnetic data. Chapter 1 supplies the Bayesian formulation that the rest of the project's inversion workflow must instantiate; Chapter 2 supplies the Monte-Carlo machinery for cases where the posterior is non-Gaussian (essentially every realistic case); Chapter 3 supplies the gradient-based optimization machinery for the Gaussian-prior special case that is the workhorse of large-scale inversion; and the appendices supply the model-space-metric discussion needed to choose a defensible parameterization. It pairs naturally with Grana, Mukerji & Doyen (2021) for the seismic-reservoir-inversion specialization, with Mavko, Mukerji & Dvorkin (2020) for the rock-physics forward models that enter the likelihood, and with Carcione (2022) for the seismic forward models in the high-fidelity FWI limit.
