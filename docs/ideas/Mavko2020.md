# Review: Mavko, Mukerji & Dvorkin (2020), The Rock Physics Handbook (3rd ed.)

## Context and contribution

*The Rock Physics Handbook: Tools for Seismic Analysis of Porous Media* by Gary Mavko, Tapan Mukerji and Jack Dvorkin is the third edition of what has been, since the first edition in 1998, the de facto reference manual of quantitative rock physics. The book is published by Cambridge University Press, runs to roughly 740 pages, and is the synthesis of three decades of work by the Stanford Rock Physics and Borehole Geophysics Project (SRB) and its industry consortium. The first edition (1998) collected and standardized the formulas that had been scattered across the rock-physics literature; the second (2009) added porous-media models, electromagnetic relations and statistical rock physics; the third (2020) substantially expands the coverage of unconventional reservoirs, organic-rich shales, digital rock physics, machine-learning-assisted property estimation, and the Backus-style upscaling of finely layered media.

The book's distinctive feature is its *format*. It is deliberately not a linear textbook. Instead, each topic is presented as a short, modular "recipe" of two to ten pages that states the physical idea, the equations in usable form, the assumptions and limits of applicability, and a worked numerical example, with references at the end of each recipe. This is the handbook a working geophysicist actually keeps on the desk: when an inversion needs a Gassmann fluid substitution, a Hashin–Shtrikman bound, a Hertz–Mindlin contact-mechanics calibration, a Backus average of a fine VTI stack, or an empirical Vp–Vs regression, the formula is there in citable, equation-numbered form. Where Bourbié–Coussy–Zinszner (1987) and Carcione (2022) are derivational monographs that prove things, *The Rock Physics Handbook* is the catalogue that *uses* those results.

The third edition also reflects a notable shift in emphasis. The 1998 edition was largely about clean sandstones and Gassmann fluid substitution for conventional bright-spot interpretation. The 2020 edition gives roughly equal weight to anisotropic shales, mixed-mineralogy unconventional reservoirs (kerogen as a third constituent), digital rock physics from micro-CT imagery, and statistical / machine-learning approaches to lithology and fluid classification — reflecting the shift in industry rock-physics workflow over the past twenty years.

## Main ideas and structural progression

**Section 1 — Basic tools.** The opening section is a self-contained reference on the mathematical infrastructure used throughout: coordinate transformations, tensor algebra for fourth-rank stiffness tensors, complex-modulus algebra for viscoelastic media, basic statistics, Monte Carlo sampling, and elementary regression. The Voigt and Kelvin notations are reconciled, and the Bond transformation for rotating a stiffness tensor between coordinate frames is given explicitly — a small thing that saves hours when one is moving between core-plug, well-log and seismic coordinate systems.

**Section 2 — Elasticity and Hooke's law.** The seven crystallographic symmetries (isotropic, hexagonal/VTI, orthorhombic, monoclinic, triclinic, plus HTI and TTI as rotated VTI cases) are tabulated with the explicit form of the stiffness matrix and the Thomsen / Tsvankin / Rüger weak-anisotropy parameters. Group, phase and energy velocity expressions for general anisotropic media are given, with the Backus average of a finely layered isotropic stack as the canonical worked example of how seismic-scale VTI emerges from sub-seismic layering. The Backus recipes are the practical bridge between log-scale measurements and seismic-scale inversion parameters and are among the most heavily used in the book.

**Section 3 — Seismic wave propagation.** Plane-wave reflection and transmission coefficients are tabulated in their full form (Zoeppritz) and in the various linearized approximations actually used in AVO inversion: Aki–Richards, Shuey, Fatti, and the Rüger HTI/orthorhombic AVAZ extensions. A separate recipe gives the Bortfeld formulation for converted-wave (PS) reflectivity. Each formula is annotated with its small-contrast / small-angle / weak-anisotropy assumptions, which makes the book unusually safe to cite in an inversion paper because one always knows what was approximated away.

**Section 4 — Effective media: bounds and mixing laws.** This is the empirical heart of the book. The Voigt–Reuss–Hill, Hashin–Shtrikman and Hashin–Shtrikman–Walpole bounds are all given for elastic moduli, with derivations and worked numerical examples for two- and three-phase mineral mixtures. A great strength of the third edition is its careful comparison of *bounds* (rigorous, narrowing to a band of allowed values) with *mixing laws* (e.g. Wood's law for fluids, Wyllie time-average) which are particular trajectories inside the bounds. Differential effective medium (DEM), self-consistent (SCA), and Kuster–Toksöz inclusion theories are given for ellipsoidal pores and cracks, and the Berryman / Hudson / Eshelby derivations are reconciled into a single notation. The user can read off, for a given porosity and mineralogy, the full envelope of physically admissible elastic responses — which is the proper way to communicate rock-physics uncertainty to an inversion downstream.

**Section 5 — Granular media.** Hertz–Mindlin contact theory is the basis for the cement-free, friction-controlled end-member of unconsolidated and weakly consolidated sediments. The chapter develops the Walton bound, the Digby cemented-grain model, Dvorkin's *stiff-sand* and *soft-sand* (modified Hashin–Shtrikman) models, and the contact-cement model. These four templates — soft-sand, stiff-sand, friable-sand and constant-cement — between them describe most reservoir sandstones, and the chapter is essentially a paint-by-numbers tutorial for fitting them to log data. The unconventional-reservoir extensions added in the third edition treat mixed sand–shale–kerogen systems and their soft-/stiff-sand calibration.

**Section 6 — Fluid effects on wave propagation.** This is the section that is most heavily cited in seismic interpretation. The recipes are Gassmann fluid substitution in its standard and anisotropic forms, the Brown–Korringa generalization for anisotropic dry frames, the patchy-saturation interpolation between Reuss and Voigt averages, Biot's high-frequency dynamic theory, Dvorkin–Mavko BISQ squirt-flow theory, and the modern White / Pride–Berryman–Harris / Müller–Gurevich mesoscopic-loss models. A separate recipe gives Batzle–Wang fluid-property formulas (brine, oil, gas) as a function of pressure, temperature, salinity and GOR, which is the input every Gassmann substitution actually needs. This section is the natural complement to Carcione (2022) Ch. 7: where Carcione derives the dispersion relations, Mavko et al. tabulates the parameters and provides the substitution recipes.

**Section 7 — Empirical relations.** A pragmatic chapter of velocity–porosity, velocity–clay and Vp–Vs regressions: Han, Castagna, Greenberg–Castagna, Krief, and various lithology- and basin-specific fits. The third edition adds organic-rich-shale regressions and explicitly flags the danger of extrapolating any of these empirical lines outside their calibration range, which is a recurring source of error in lithology and fluid prediction.

**Section 8 — Flow, diffusion and electrical properties.** Permeability–porosity relations (Kozeny–Carman, Timur, Coates–Dumanoir, NMR-derived Schlumberger / Coates), Archie resistivity, dual-water and Waxman–Smits clay models, and the seismoelectric / electrokinetic coupling relations needed for coupled seismic-EM inversion. The treatment is short but is sufficient to use these models with the elastic ones in a joint inversion.

**Section 9 — Statistical rock physics and machine learning (new in the 3rd ed.).** A substantial new section that treats the rock-physics calibration problem in a Bayesian / data-driven framework: rock-physics templates, prior-conditioned probabilistic Gassmann inversion, classification trees and random forests for lithology and fluid prediction, and Gaussian-process regression for log-property prediction. This section codifies what the SRB consortium has been teaching for ten years and brings the *Handbook* in line with current quantitative-interpretation practice.

**Section 10 — Digital rock physics (new / expanded in the 3rd ed.).** Numerical computation of effective elastic and transport properties from segmented micro-CT images: FFT-based homogenization, finite-element pore-scale simulation, lattice-Boltzmann permeability, and the digital-rock workflow from imaging through segmentation to upscaling. This section is the closest thing in the literature to a single-source tutorial on the whole digital-rock pipeline and is one of the main reasons to upgrade from the 2009 to the 2020 edition.

**Appendices.** A standardized table of mineral elastic constants and densities (quartz, feldspar, calcite, dolomite, anhydrite, halite, clay-mineral end-members, kerogen, ice, hydrate), fluid constants from the Batzle–Wang relations tabulated, and a compact list of common stiffness-tensor algebra identities — all in citable form.

## Significance and limitations

The book's enduring value is that it is the single reference where the entire rock-physics catalogue — bounds, mixing laws, inclusion theories, contact-mechanics models, Gassmann/Biot fluid effects, empirical regressions, electrical and transport relations, statistical and digital methods — is present in one notation, with explicit equations and stated limits of applicability. Every quantitative-interpretation paper of the past decade essentially uses some subset of its recipes. The new statistical and digital-rock sections in the 2020 edition make it the up-to-date reference for the way rock-physics analysis is now done in industry and in the SRB-trained academic community.

Three limitations are worth flagging. First, this is a *handbook*, not a derivation; the user is expected to consult Carcione (2022), Coussy (2004), or the cited primary papers for the underlying physics. Many recipes give the formula without the derivation. Second, although the third edition has expanded its anisotropic and attenuating-medium content, it remains primarily an *isotropic-elastic and Gassmann-substitution* reference: attenuation anisotropy, full-frequency-dependent viscoelastic AVO, and anisotropic poroelasticity are present but treated more briefly than in Carcione 2022. Third, the modular recipe format means that the relationships *between* models — when does Hashin–Shtrikman reduce to Voigt, why do soft-sand and Hertz–Mindlin agree in a particular limit, how does Brown–Korringa reduce to Gassmann under isotropy — must be inferred by the reader cross-referencing recipes, rather than being developed by a single unifying argument. For a unifying argument the right companion is Mukerji's lecture notes or Avseth, Mukerji & Mavko (2005) *Quantitative Seismic Interpretation*.

For our project the book is the natural reference whenever the inversion or forward simulation requires (i) *Gassmann or Biot fluid substitution* in any of its standard or extended forms; (ii) calibrated *granular-media moduli* (soft-sand, stiff-sand, contact-cement) for unconsolidated and weakly consolidated reservoirs; (iii) *Backus averaging* of fine-layered logs to seismic-scale VTI parameters; (iv) *empirical Vp–Vs / Vp–porosity regressions* for lithology and fluid prediction; or (v) *bounds and uncertainty envelopes* on effective elastic moduli given mineral and fluid uncertainty. It pairs naturally with Bourbié–Coussy–Zinszner (1987) for the derivation side of Gassmann–Biot, with Carcione (2022) for anisotropic and attenuating extensions, and with Avseth–Mukerji–Mavko (2005) for the seismic-inversion side.
