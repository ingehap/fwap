# Review: Červený (2001), Seismic Ray Theory

## Context and contribution

*Seismic Ray Theory* by Vlastislav Červený, published by Cambridge University Press in 2001 and running 722 pages, is the definitive monograph on the high-frequency asymptotic treatment of seismic wave propagation in laterally inhomogeneous, anisotropic media. The book consolidates roughly forty years of work by the Prague (Charles University) seismic-ray-theory school — Červený, Pšenčík, Klimeš, Hron, Popov, and their collaborators — whose successive *Geophysical Journal*, *Studia Geophysica et Geodaetica*, and SEG-monograph contributions defined the modern formulation of ray theory. Before this volume, the working seismologist learned ray theory from Červený, Molotkov & Pšenčík's earlier *Ray Method in Seismology* (Charles University Press, 1977) and from Aki & Richards (1980) Chapter 9; both treatments are now superseded by the present, unified, fully developed monograph.

The book's distinctive contribution is to develop seismic ray theory as a *complete asymptotic theory*, including not only kinematic (traveltime) ray tracing but also dynamic (amplitude) ray tracing, paraxial ray approximations, Gaussian-beam summation, anisotropic-medium extensions, and ray-perturbation theory in a single notational framework. Where the typical textbook treatment of ray theory (e.g., Aki & Richards Ch. 9; Shearer 2019) stops at the eikonal equation and qualitative amplitude considerations, Červený develops the full ray-amplitude calculation including geometrical spreading, transmission/reflection coefficients along the ray, and the Maslov index for caustic phase shifts; treats two-point ray tracing as a boundary-value problem with paraxial-ray sensitivity kernels; extends every result to general anisotropic media; and supplies the Gaussian-beam-summation technology that overcomes the caustic singularities of classical ray theory.

The book also occupies a particular methodological niche. Compared with full-waveform finite-difference or pseudospectral modelling (Carcione 2022 Ch. 9), ray theory is asymptotic — it is exact only in the high-frequency limit — but it is *cheap* (orders of magnitude faster than finite differences), *interpretable* (rays correspond to physical wavefronts), and *invertible* (the ray equations can be linearized and inverted, which underlies traveltime tomography and prestack Kirchhoff migration). For the working exploration geophysicist, Červený 2001 is the reference behind ray-based traveltime tomography, prestack depth migration (Kirchhoff), AVO modelling along ray paths, and the analytical treatment of microseismic-event location in heterogeneous media.

## Main ideas and structural progression

**Chapter 1 — Introduction.** A short orientation chapter that positions ray theory between geometric optics and wave theory, distinguishes kinematic from dynamic ray tracing, and previews the asymptotic-expansion programme that the rest of the book develops.

**Chapter 2 — The elastodynamic equation and its simple solutions.** A self-contained recapitulation of the elastodynamic equation in isotropic and anisotropic media, of plane waves and the Christoffel equation, and of the basic kinds of body and surface waves. The chapter sets the notation and the modal decomposition (qP, qS1, qS2 in anisotropic media; P, SV, SH in isotropic) used throughout, and is the only chapter that overlaps substantially with Aki & Richards or Carcione.

**Chapter 3 — Seismic rays and traveltimes.** The kinematic core. The asymptotic-ray-method (ARM) ansatz u(x, t) = U(x) f(t − τ(x)) is substituted into the elastodynamic equation; the leading-order condition gives the *eikonal equation* for the traveltime function τ; and the *ray equations* are derived as the characteristics of the eikonal equation. The chapter develops ray tracing in vertically and laterally inhomogeneous, isotropic and anisotropic media; in Cartesian, cylindrical and spherical coordinates; and via the *initial-value formulation* (shoot a ray from a source with a chosen take-off direction) and the *boundary-value formulation* (two-point ray tracing between a fixed source and a fixed receiver). The two-point problem is treated via shooting methods, bending methods, and the wavefront-construction method, with stability analysis for each.

A subsidiary section in this chapter treats *interface conditions*: when a ray hits an interface between two media, Snell's law (with the anisotropic group/phase distinction made carefully) determines the reflected and transmitted ray directions, and the *Fresnel zone* around the geometric ray gives the spatial scale over which the ray approximation is locally accurate. The Fresnel-zone construction reappears in Chapters 5 and 6 as the natural smoothing length of ray-amplitude predictions.

**Chapter 4 — Dynamic ray tracing. Paraxial ray methods.** The chapter develops the *paraxial* ray approximation: the linearized behaviour of nearby rays around a central ray. The dynamic-ray-tracing system is derived as the linearization of the ray equations, giving a 4×4 (in 2-D) or 6×6 (in 3-D) matrix ODE for the paraxial-ray vector along the central ray. Solutions of this system yield the *ray Jacobian* J, whose vanishing locates caustics, and the *geometrical spreading factor* L = √|J| that governs the amplitude attenuation along the ray. The chapter develops in detail the two principal paraxial parameterizations — Cartesian coordinates centred on the ray, and ray-centred coordinates — and reconciles them. The paraxial-ray approximation is the technical foundation of every subsequent chapter: ray amplitudes (Ch. 5), Gaussian beams (Ch. 6), and ray-perturbation methods (Ch. 8) all build on it.

**Chapter 5 — Ray amplitudes.** The next-order asymptotic condition, derived from the elastodynamic equation, gives the *transport equation* for the ray amplitude. Solving the transport equation along a ray expresses the amplitude as the product of a source factor, the inverse geometrical-spreading factor 1/L from Chapter 4, transmission and reflection coefficients at each interface, and a Maslov-index phase shift at each caustic the ray crosses. The chapter develops all of these factors explicitly for general anisotropic media, including the polarization vector and its parallel transport along the ray. The treatment of the *transmission/reflection coefficients* connects directly to the Aki–Richards / Zoeppritz machinery of conventional seismic AVO and shows how to do AVO modelling along a curved ray in a heterogeneous medium — which is the basis of pre-stack ray-based AVO migration.

**Chapter 6 — Ray synthetic seismograms.** Putting kinematics and dynamics together. Given a source, a model, and a receiver, the chapter constructs the synthetic seismogram as the sum over all rays connecting source and receiver, each weighted by the amplitude of Chapter 5 and delayed by the traveltime of Chapter 3. The chapter treats *multiple arrivals* (a single source–receiver pair can be connected by many distinct rays — direct, reflected, head, diffracted), the catalogue of *caustic phase shifts*, and the *failure modes* of ray theory at caustics and shadow zones. The *Gaussian-beam summation* method is developed here as the standard fix for caustic singularities: the ray itself is replaced by a Gaussian-modulated bundle whose paraxial width is controlled by the dynamic-ray system of Chapter 4, and the synthetic seismogram is the integral of these beams over the take-off-direction sphere. Gaussian-beam summation is the technology underlying modern wave-equation-equivalent ray methods, including beam migration and beam-domain inversion.

**Chapter 7 — Seismic ray method in anisotropic media.** Although the preceding chapters introduce anisotropy as it is needed, Chapter 7 is the systematic treatment. The Christoffel equation in anisotropic media has three distinct eigenvalues / mode types (qP, qS1, qS2), and the ray equations must be solved for each mode separately. The chapter develops the special-case anisotropic geometries — VTI, HTI, orthorhombic, monoclinic, triclinic — and the *coupling* phenomena that arise when two qS modes have nearly degenerate slowness (the so-called *singularity* problem, where ray theory breaks down and full coupling between the two modes must be retained). The chapter is the reference for ray theory in fractured-reservoir and shale contexts where anisotropy is the dominant rock-physical property; it complements Tsvankin & Grechka (2011) on the anisotropic-inversion side.

**Chapter 8 — Ray method with the Hamiltonian formalism. Ray perturbation methods.** A more advanced chapter that reformulates the ray method using the canonical Hamiltonian / symplectic framework of classical mechanics. The Hamiltonian formulation makes contact with classical mechanics, simplifies the derivation of conservation laws, and provides the natural setting for *ray-perturbation methods* — first-order Born-style sensitivity of traveltime and amplitude to perturbations in the model. The ray-perturbation kernels developed here are the technology behind sensitivity-kernel traveltime tomography (the so-called "finite-frequency" or "banana–doughnut" tomography of Dahlen, Hung & Nolet) and behind first-order anisotropic-perturbation tomography.

**Appendices.** Substantial appendices treat the Hamiltonian formalism, the algebraic and tensor manipulations needed for general anisotropic media, the various coordinate systems used (Cartesian, ray-centred, wavefront-orthonormal), and the numerical algorithms (Runge–Kutta variants, two-point shooting / bending) that the practical implementation of ray tracing requires. The appendices are the place where the working implementer of a ray-tracing code goes to settle conventions.

## Significance and limitations

The book's enduring value is that it is the single reference where the complete asymptotic theory of high-frequency seismic wave propagation — eikonal, transport, paraxial, anisotropic-medium, Gaussian-beam, perturbation — is developed in one consistent notation with full mathematical rigour and with explicit attention to the numerical-implementation issues that face the practitioner. Every modern ray-based modelling, migration and tomography algorithm cites this book for the underlying theory; the Prague ray-tracing software (SEIS83, ANRAY, NORSAR-3D) and most academic and industrial ray-tracers implement the algorithms in the form Červený gives them here.

The book is also the natural complement to the wave-equation-based forward-modelling literature. Where full-waveform finite-difference modelling (Carcione 2022 Ch. 9) is exact but expensive and opaque, ray theory is approximate but cheap and interpretable; the two approaches are complementary, and the modern practitioner uses each in the regime where it is appropriate (ray theory for traveltime tomography, prestack ray-Kirchhoff migration, microseismic event location; full waveform for FWI, RTM, and the high-fidelity simulation of complex models).

Three limitations are worth flagging for the contemporary reader. First, the book is fundamentally an *asymptotic-theory* monograph: in the low-frequency, sub-wavelength regime where caustics and shadow zones occur, the ray method genuinely breaks down, and the Gaussian-beam summation of Chapter 6 is a partial — but only partial — fix. For low-frequency / wavelength-scale heterogeneity the modern practitioner uses full-waveform modelling, finite-frequency sensitivity kernels (Dahlen et al. 2000) or hybrid ray-Born methods (Beydoun & Mendes 1989). Second, the book predates the routine use of *adjoint-state* / FWI methods and of sensitivity-kernel tomography in the modern form; the perturbation-theory chapter (Ch. 8) supplies the formal foundation but not the modern computational practice. Third, the book's treatment of *attenuation* and of *coupled poroelastic* media is brief; for these the right complement is Carcione (2022). None of these qualifies the central claim that this is the definitive monograph on seismic ray theory and the foundation of every ray-based seismic-imaging code.

For our project the book is the natural reference whenever the forward problem can be treated in the high-frequency / ray limit: Chapter 3 supplies the kinematic ray-tracing machinery underlying traveltime tomography and refraction-seismic interpretation; Chapter 4 supplies the dynamic-ray-tracing technology for geometrical spreading and paraxial sensitivities; Chapter 5 supplies the ray-amplitude calculation underlying ray-based AVO modelling in laterally heterogeneous media; Chapter 6 supplies the Gaussian-beam-summation machinery for caustic-stable ray-based synthetic seismograms and beam migration; Chapter 7 supplies the anisotropic-ray-tracing extensions for VTI / HTI / orthorhombic reservoirs; and Chapter 8 supplies the ray-perturbation kernels for first-order sensitivity-based tomography. It pairs naturally with Aki & Richards (1980) for the broader theoretical-seismology context, with Carcione (2022) for the full-waveform / wave-equation side of forward modelling, and with Tsvankin & Grechka (2011) for the anisotropic-AVO and parameter-estimation side.
