# Review: Lee, Kanamori, Jennings & Kisslinger (Eds.) (2002–2003), International Handbook of Earthquake and Engineering Seismology

## Context and contribution

The *International Handbook of Earthquake and Engineering Seismology* — edited by William H. K. Lee (USGS, retired), Hiroo Kanamori (Caltech), Paul C. Jennings (Caltech), and Carl Kisslinger (CIRES, University of Colorado), and sponsored by the International Association of Seismology and Physics of the Earth's Interior (IASPEI) — is a two-part, multi-author reference handbook published by Academic Press / Elsevier as Volumes 81A (2002) and 81B (2003) of the *International Geophysics Series*. The two parts together run roughly 1,945 pages, comprise more than eighty chapters by some 200 contributing authors, and were issued with accompanying CD-ROMs containing an additional set of supplementary chapters and the *IASPEI Software Library* (ISL) — the FORTRAN / C source code for the international community's standard traveltime, location, magnitude, and observatory algorithms. As a single bound reference the handbook is the definitive 2000s-era handbook of seismology in the same role that Aki & Richards (1980) holds for theoretical seismology and that Engdahl (1984) held for observatory algorithms.

The handbook's distinctive contribution is not original research but *comprehensive disciplinary coverage*: an authoritative review of every sub-discipline of earthquake and engineering seismology, written by an internationally recognized expert in each sub-discipline, in a uniform editorial format. Where Aki & Richards is a single-perspective textbook of theoretical seismology, and Engdahl (1984) is a code-only compendium, the Lee–Kanamori–Jennings–Kisslinger handbook is the field's encyclopaedia — the place a researcher entering an unfamiliar sub-discipline (strong-motion seismology, liquefaction, seismic-hazard mapping, observatory practice, ambient-noise tomography, real-time earthquake warning) goes for a self-contained twenty- to forty-page survey with a current reference list. The handbook is also the principal published record of IASPEI's commission work in the 1990s — earthquake-source nomenclature, magnitude-scale standardization, traveltime-table conventions, and the Manual of Seismological Observatory Practice.

A second contribution, important in its own right, is the CD-ROM software distribution. The IASPEI Software Library packaged with the handbook — most notably the Buland–Chapman traveltime computation, the iasp91 / ak135 reference earth models, the standard HYPO71 / HYPOELLIPSE / LOCSAT location codes, and the standard magnitude and focal-mechanism algorithms — is the operational successor to Engdahl (1984) and the immediate predecessor of the modern ObsPy / SeisComP / open-source ecosystem. For roughly a decade after publication, "as documented in the IASPEI Handbook" was the standard reference for the operational definition of these algorithms.

## Main ideas and structural progression

The handbook is divided into ten parts spanning the two physical volumes; the brief outline below follows the editors' published table of contents.

**Part I — History.** A short opening part on the history of seismology and its institutions: the founding of IASPEI, the World-Wide Standardized Seismograph Network (WWSSN), the Federation of Digital Broadband Seismograph Networks (FDSN), and the evolution from analogue to digital observatory instrumentation. The chapter on Beno Gutenberg and Charles Richter is a notable historical contribution; the part as a whole serves as orientation rather than as a research resource.

**Part II — Theoretical seismology.** A condensed twentieth-century-perspective review of the theoretical apparatus also covered in Aki & Richards (1980): elastodynamics and the seismic Green's tensor; ray theory and surface-wave theory; free oscillations of the Earth and normal-mode summation; mode coupling and lateral heterogeneity; numerical methods (finite-difference, pseudo-spectral, finite-element, spectral-element) for the wave equation; and the foundations of seismic tomography. The chapters are shorter than the equivalent material in Aki & Richards but more current — they incorporate, in particular, the 1990s developments in spectral-element forward modelling (Komatitsch, Tromp) and finite-frequency / banana-doughnut sensitivity-kernel tomography (Dahlen, Hung, Nolet) that postdate the original Aki & Richards volume.

**Part III — Observation of earthquakes.** Observatory and network seismology in its modern, fully digital form: digital seismometer design and calibration; signal processing; phase picking, automatic and manual; the moveable-mean and STA/LTA detection algorithms; magnitude scales (mb, mB, MS, ML, Mw) with the IASPEI Working-Group recommendations for their standardization; earthquake catalogues and bulletins (ISC, NEIC, JMA); the moment-tensor inversion machinery (Global CMT project) in its post-1981 operational form. The chapter on magnitude scales is the citation behind essentially every magnitude-correction effort of the post-2003 period.

**Part IV — Earthquake source.** Earthquake-source physics in twenty chapters: the kinematic source description (rupture velocity, slip distribution, directivity), the dynamic source description (stress drop, fracture energy, radiation efficiency), the source-spectrum scaling and self-similarity, the fault-mechanics laboratory and theoretical literature (rate-and-state friction, slip pulses), induced and triggered seismicity, slow-slip phenomena, deep earthquakes and their distinct source physics, and the very-large-earthquake / megathrust regime. The handbook's chapter on the 1960 Chile and 1964 Alaska earthquakes, with their reanalysis using digital archival data, is one of the more cited individual contributions; subsequent revisions (after the 2004 Sumatra, 2011 Tōhoku, and 2010 Maule earthquakes) appear only in successor literature.

**Part V — Seismic hazards.** Probabilistic seismic hazard analysis (PSHA), deterministic hazard, site effects, ground-motion prediction equations (GMPEs), earthquake catalogues for hazard assessment, declustering algorithms, magnitude–frequency relations (Gutenberg–Richter b-values), and earthquake recurrence on individual faults. The contributions by Cornell, Reiter, McGuire and others codify the PSHA framework that subsequently underpins national seismic-hazard maps and the design ground motions in earthquake-engineering codes worldwide.

**Part VI — Strong-motion seismology.** Strong-motion instrumentation, networks (K-NET, KiK-net, COSMOS, ESM), record processing (baseline correction, integration to velocity and displacement, filter design), characterization (PGA, PGV, PGD, response spectra, Arias intensity), and stochastic and physics-based strong-motion simulation. The chapter on stochastic strong-motion simulation (Boore's stochastic method) is a frequently-cited operational reference.

**Part VII — Engineering seismology.** The earthquake-engineering interface: structural response to ground motion, performance-based earthquake engineering, soil–structure interaction, liquefaction, slope failure under seismic loading, and tsunami generation and propagation. This is the part that distinguishes the handbook from purely Earth-science seismology references; it is the reason the volume is held in earthquake-engineering as well as in seismology libraries.

**Part VIII — Selected topics.** A mix of topics that did not fit neatly into the preceding parts: ambient-noise correlation and Green's-function retrieval (in its 2002–2003 form, before its post-2005 explosion); ocean-bottom seismology; volcanic seismology; near-real-time earthquake warning; and educational outreach. The ambient-noise chapter, although superseded by post-2005 developments, is the first textbook-style treatment of the technique.

**Part IX — National reports.** Some forty chapters, one per country or region, summarising the state of seismology in each — networks, institutions, hazard maps, recent research priorities. These chapters are largely historical interest now but are useful when reconstructing the regional state of practice in the early 2000s.

**Part X — Personalia, biographies, and bibliographies.** Biographical and bibliographical resources for the discipline, including biographies of key twentieth-century figures and a comprehensive bibliography. The accompanying CD-ROMs contain an extended set of supplementary chapters, the IASPEI Software Library source code, and digital archives of the *Manual of Seismological Observatory Practice* and related IASPEI publications.

## Significance and limitations

The handbook's enduring value is as a *first-reference encyclopaedia* of earthquake and engineering seismology. For any sub-discipline of the field, the corresponding chapter is the standard place to start: it gives the methodology, the operational definitions, the canonical citation network, and the leading authors as of the early 2000s. The CD-ROM software collection makes the operational definitions reproducible, which is why citations of the IASPEI Software Library through this venue remain common. The standardized magnitude-scale recommendations (Bormann, Wendt et al.) and the standardized observatory-practice manual that the IASPEI Working Groups produced and the handbook codifies are the field's most-used disciplinary conventions.

Three limitations are worth flagging for the contemporary reader. First, the handbook is a *2002–2003 snapshot* of the field; the post-2003 transformations — the 2004 Sumatra–Andaman, 2008 Wenchuan, 2010 Maule, 2011 Tōhoku and 2023 Türkiye–Syria earthquakes; the explosion of ambient-noise tomography after Shapiro et al. (2005); the rise of distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) and the rapid expansion of induced-seismicity studies linked to fluid injection — are absent because they postdate publication. Second, the *engineering* and *strong-motion* parts are denser and longer than the chapters covering modern computational seismology (full-waveform inversion, adjoint tomography, machine-learning phase pickers); a reader looking for the latter will find them only briefly treated in Part II and will need to consult more recent literature (Tromp 2020 *Seismic Wavefield Imaging of Earth's Interior*; the Annual Reviews series on FWI). Third, the multi-author handbook format means the *depth* of treatment is uneven across chapters: some chapters are textbook-quality reviews and others are little more than annotated bibliographies. None of these qualifies the central claim that, for the disciplinary coverage and operational definitions of early-2000s seismology, this is the standard reference.

For our project the handbook is the natural reference whenever the question is *what is the standard observatory or community algorithm for X*: traveltime computation via iasp91 / ak135 (Part II / Part III, with the IASPEI Software Library source); earthquake-magnitude conventions (Part III; the IASPEI Magnitude Working Group recommendations); earthquake-location codes (Part III; ISL HYPO71 / HYPOELLIPSE / LOCSAT source); moment-tensor inversion (Part III–IV); probabilistic seismic-hazard analysis (Part V); and the strong-motion record-processing pipeline (Part VI). It pairs naturally with Aki & Richards (1980) and Carcione (2022) on the theoretical-seismology side, with Engdahl (1984) for the historical-precedent of the algorithm documentation, with Bormann's *NMSOP-2* (2012) for the modern observatory-practice manual, and with the post-2003 monographs (Tromp 2020; Ringler et al. 2022) for the developments after publication.
