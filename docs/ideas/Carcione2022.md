# Review: Carcione (2022), Wave Fields in Real Media: Wave Propagation in Anisotropic, Anelastic, Porous and Electromagnetic Media

## Context and contribution

This is the fourth, substantially expanded edition of José M. Carcione's standard monograph, published by Elsevier in 2022 and running to roughly 880 pages. The first edition appeared in 2001 as Volume 31 of the *Handbook of Geophysical Exploration: Seismic Exploration* series; the second (2007) added a complete chapter on the electromagnetic analogy; the third (2014) added poroelastic-electromagnetic couplings and substantially updated the numerical chapter; the fourth (2022) integrates a further decade of work — fractional-derivative attenuation models, double- and triple-porosity Biot theory, mesoscopic-loss / wave-induced fluid flow (WIFF), thermoelasticity coupled to Biot, and a much enlarged treatment of numerical schemes for low-symmetry, attenuating, porous media.

What distinguishes the book from its competitors (Aki & Richards; Auld; Bourbié, Coussy & Zinszner; Mavko, Mukerji & Dvorkin; Tsvankin) is its insistence on treating *real* media — i.e., simultaneously anisotropic *and* anelastic *and* porous, with electromagnetic propagation handled as a formal analogue — within a single notation and a single set of mathematical tools (plane-wave analysis, complex slowness surfaces, Green's functions, time-domain memory variables). Where Tsvankin's monographs treat anisotropy in essentially elastic media, and Bourbié et al. treat poroelasticity in essentially isotropic media, Carcione's book is the place readers go when the rock is genuinely all of these things at once. The author was for thirty years at OGS Trieste and is the primary developer of the OGS modelling codes, and the book carries the stamp of that working numerical practice throughout: nearly every theoretical chapter ends with a worked synthetic example tied to the algorithms of Chapter 9.

## Main ideas and structural progression

**Anisotropic elastic media (Ch. 1).** The opening chapter develops the stiffness/compliance tensor, the Christoffel equation and the slowness, group-velocity and energy-velocity surfaces for the seven crystallographic symmetry classes of interest in geophysics — isotropic, transversely isotropic (VTI, HTI, TTI), orthorhombic, monoclinic and triclinic. The treatment is built around the energy-balance equation and the Kelvin–Christoffel decomposition, which become the recurring tool of the rest of the book. Reflection/transmission, SH–qP–qSV mode conversions, and the kiss/wedge/intersection singularities of qS sheets are worked in detail, with the orthorhombic case treated as the canonical low-symmetry example. The chapter sets the conventions (Voigt vs. Kelvin notation, Thomsen vs. Tsvankin parameters) that govern the remainder of the volume.

**Viscoelasticity and the time-domain memory variable (Chs. 2–3).** Carcione's signature contribution to the field is the systematic use of *memory variables* to convert the convolutional stress–strain relations of a viscoelastic medium into an enlarged first-order ODE system that can be marched in time as cheaply as an elastic system. Chapter 2 builds the relaxation-function / creep-function machinery for the standard linear solid, the generalized Maxwell/Zener model with L relaxation mechanisms, the constant-Q (Kjartansson) model, and — new in the fourth edition — the fractional Zener and Cole–Cole models that give algebraically decaying creep at long times. Chapter 3 specializes to isotropic anelastic media, develops dispersion relations and complex velocities, and shows how Q-anisotropy and velocity-anisotropy can be made to coexist in a single Zener-type framework. The closed treatment of *constant-Q in the time domain* via a small number of Zener mechanisms (typically L = 3) is one of the book's most-cited practical results and underlies most production-grade Q-FD codes in the seismic community.

**Anisotropic anelastic media (Ch. 4).** Chapter 4 fuses Chapters 1 and 3: the elastic stiffness tensor is replaced by a complex, frequency-dependent stiffness tensor whose imaginary part encodes a possibly direction-dependent Q. The chapter derives the modified Christoffel equation, defines the *energy* (as opposed to phase or group) velocity that governs wavefront arrivals in a dissipative anisotropic medium, and shows that for general triclinic-attenuating media the three quasi-modes are no longer mutually orthogonal — a subtlety that matters for AVO and for full-waveform inversion in attenuating shales. The Q-Thomsen parameters of Zhu & Tsvankin (2006) are obtained as a weak-anisotropy, weak-attenuation limit of the general formulae developed here.

**Reciprocity, Green's functions and plane-wave reflection (Chs. 5–6).** Chapter 5 develops the reciprocity principle in its most general form — anisotropic, anelastic, poroelastic, electromagnetic — and shows that the source-receiver interchange that underlies seismic interferometry, surface-wave Green's-function retrieval, and the adjoint state used in FWI all flow from a single Betti-Rayleigh-style identity. Chapter 6 returns to plane-wave reflection and transmission, now systematically extended to interfaces between low-symmetry, attenuating, possibly porous media. Brewster, critical and Stoneley angles are tracked through this generalization, and the chapter recovers the Rüger AVAZ formulae and the Bortfeld/Aki–Richards approximations as special cases. New in the fourth edition is a careful treatment of inhomogeneous and *leaky* plane waves at the interface between two attenuating anisotropic half-spaces.

**Biot poroelasticity (Ch. 7).** This is the longest and most expanded chapter of the book. The classical Biot (1956a,b; 1962) theory is rederived from Lagrangian first principles, and then extended along three axes that have become central in the last twenty years. (i) *Mesoscopic loss / wave-induced fluid flow (WIFF):* patchy saturation, dual-porosity and triple-porosity (White; Dutta–Odé; Pride–Berryman–Harris; Ba) models that explain attenuation peaks in the 10 Hz – 1 kHz band that classical Biot theory cannot. (ii) *Anisotropic Biot theory:* the porous-medium stiffness, permeability and Biot coefficient become tensors, with the appropriate Christoffel equation for fast P, slow P (Biot's second wave) and quasi-S. (iii) *Coupled fields:* thermoelastic Biot (Lord–Shulman thermal-relaxation), electrokinetic / seismoelectric coupling via the Pride equations, and poroelastic–electromagnetic conversion. The chapter ends with a careful discussion of the homogenization regimes — Gassmann low frequency, Biot inertial high frequency, mesoscopic intermediate — and which to use when.

**The acoustic–electromagnetic analogy (Ch. 8).** A whole chapter is devoted to the formal mapping between Maxwell's equations and the equations of viscoelasticity / poroelasticity in two dimensions. The point is not philosophical but practical: a code written for SH-wave propagation in a VTI viscoelastic medium can, with no change of structure and a relabelling of variables, simulate TM electromagnetic propagation in a dispersive, conductive, magnetic medium. The analogy is pushed all the way to ground-penetrating radar, controlled-source EM, and magnetotellurics, and unifies the Cole–Cole dielectric relaxation used in GPR/CSEM with the Zener mechanical relaxation of Chapter 2.

**Numerical methods (Ch. 9).** The final chapter is essentially a self-contained textbook on numerical wave propagation: explicit finite differences (staggered grids, Lebedev grids for general anisotropy, rotated staggered for fractured media), pseudospectral Fourier and Chebyshev schemes, finite-element / spectral-element methods, time-stepping by Runge–Kutta and by the rapid-expansion / Chebyshev integrator, perfectly matched layers, and free-surface conditions on rugose topography. Stability and dispersion are analyzed in the unified setting of the previous chapters, so the reader sees explicitly why, e.g., the Biot slow wave forces an extremely small time step and how to relax that constraint with implicit or splitting schemes. The fourth edition adds a substantial section on memory-variable schemes for fractional viscoelasticity, which is genuinely new material relative to the third edition.

## Significance and limitations

The book's enduring value is that it is the single reference text where a working forward-modelling or inversion practitioner can find a *consistent* and *implementable* treatment of every loss and anisotropy mechanism that matters for real rocks — VTI/orthorhombic stiffness, intrinsic and WIFF attenuation, Biot's two compressional modes, anisotropic permeability, and the electromagnetic counterparts — together with the numerical recipes to simulate them. The notation is uniform across nine chapters, the worked examples are reproducible, and the references at chapter level are extraordinarily thorough; for many subtopics (notably the history of constant-Q modelling, of the Biot slow wave, and of the acoustic–EM analogy) the historical appendices and the chapter-end notes are the most complete in the literature.

Three limitations are worth noting. First, the book is fundamentally a *forward-modelling* and *plane-wave* monograph: full-waveform inversion, time-lapse inversion, and machine-learning-assisted parameter estimation are mentioned but not developed, even though they are now the locus of activity in attenuating-anisotropic media (e.g., Q-FWI in VTI shales; viscoelastic FWI with mesoscopic loss). Second, the rock-physics side — pore-scale digital rock physics, micromechanical effective-medium theory, and stress-sensitivity — is treated mostly as boundary conditions on the macroscopic Biot parameters rather than as a subject in itself; for that, the book should be paired with Mavko, Mukerji & Dvorkin (2020). Third, the production is occasionally uneven: notation collisions between chapters (especially between the EM and porous chapters) and a few persistent typographical errors in formulae carry over from previous editions. None of these qualifies the central claim that, as of 2022, this is the standard reference for wave propagation in real geological media.

For our project the book is the natural backbone reference whenever the forward problem involves anisotropy, attenuation or porosity in combination. Chapter 4 supplies the framework for attenuating-anisotropic borehole modelling; Chapter 7 supplies the dual/triple-porosity machinery needed for sonic-frequency attenuation interpretation in fractured carbonates; and Chapter 9 supplies the staggered-grid / Lebedev FD and PML constructions used by essentially every production borehole-acoustic simulator.
