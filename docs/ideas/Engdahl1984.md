# Review: Engdahl (Ed.) (1984), Documentation of Earthquake Algorithms

## Context and contribution

*Documentation of Earthquake Algorithms*, edited by E. R. (Bob) Engdahl and published in 1984 as Report SE-35 of the World Data Center A for Solid Earth Geophysics in Boulder, Colorado, is a multi-author technical compendium that collects, in a single bound volume, the source code, mathematical documentation, and operational notes for a representative sample of the FORTRAN algorithms then in use at observatory and research seismology centres. The volume belongs to a specific 1980s genre — the World Data Center "documentation reports" published in cooperation with the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) National Geophysical Data Center, the U.S. Geological Survey, and the international IASPEI / FDSN community — whose purpose was to make production seismological code citeable, redistributable, and reproducible at a time when network protocols, version control, and online code repositories did not exist.

The book is editorially organised rather than authored: Engdahl, who was then at the U.S. National Earthquake Information Service (NEIS) and would later move to CIRES at the University of Colorado, served as the assembler and editor of contributions written by the original code authors (Červený & Pšenčík for ray tracing, Lee and Lahr for earthquake-location algorithms in the HYPO71/HYPOELLIPSE family, contributors from the IASPEI Commission on Seismological Observation and Interpretation for traveltime and magnitude algorithms, and others). The result is a reference whose value derives more from its archival role — preserving the operational definitions of the working observatory algorithms of the early 1980s — than from any single authorial argument. In subsequent literature it is cited as the canonical documentation venue for several algorithms whose primary publications were too brief to specify the working code: Červený & Pšenčík's SEIS83 ray-tracing program (cited in *Seismic Ray Theory*, Červený 2001) is the example most familiar to exploration geophysicists.

The compendium fills the operational gap between the *physical* description of earthquake-related algorithms (as one finds in Aki & Richards 1980 or in primary research papers) and the *executable* implementation that observatory practice requires. Its successors in this role are the IASPEI Software Library (Buland & Chapman 1983; Schweitzer 2007), the IRIS/SEED format documentation, the ObsPy Python toolkit (Beyreuther et al. 2010), and the modern GitHub-hosted code repositories of the SAGE / IRIS-EarthScope facility.

## Main ideas and structural progression

Because the volume is a documentation compilation, its structure is by *algorithm*, with each contribution self-contained. Contemporary citations and reviews suggest that the contents grouped naturally into four families.

**Earthquake-location algorithms.** The classical Geiger-style hypocenter-inversion programs — HYPO71 (Lee & Lahr 1975), HYPOELLIPSE (Lahr 1980), and the early NEIS / ISC global-location code that Engdahl himself worked on — are documented with their input/output formats, iteration controls, weighting schemes, station-correction handling, and explicit closed-form expressions for the Jacobian of the traveltime function with respect to the four unknowns (origin time, latitude, longitude, depth). Convergence criteria, divergence diagnostics, and the treatment of phases for which the partial derivatives are singular (vertical-incidence at the source, source–station distance much greater than depth) are spelled out. For users who have only seen the Geiger algorithm in summary, these documentation chapters are still the cleanest practical exposition.

**Traveltime computation.** Algorithms for the Jeffreys–Bullen and Herrin spherical-earth traveltime tables, with explicit derivatives needed by the location codes, and the bilinear interpolation schemes used in the working code. The volume predates the IASP91 (Kennett & Engdahl 1991) and ak135 (Kennett, Engdahl & Buland 1995) reference earth models — both of which were later edited or co-authored by Engdahl himself — but it preserves the documentation of the spherical-earth ray-tracing algorithms that those later models would supersede. The Červený–Pšenčík SEIS83 2-D laterally-varying ray-tracing program, which gives the ray-tracing engine used in much subsequent exploration and refraction-seismology work, is documented in this volume; it is the contribution most cited by exploration geophysicists.

**Magnitude and source-parameter algorithms.** Body-wave (mb), surface-wave (MS) and local (ML) magnitude algorithms, with the attenuation correction tables, the instrument-response deconvolution conventions, and the peak-amplitude-picking procedures. Source-spectrum and corner-frequency estimation algorithms — the implementation side of the Brune (1970) and Madariaga (1976) source models reviewed in Aki & Richards Chapters 10 and 15 — are documented with their windowing, tapering and noise-correction defaults. The documentation of *station corrections* and *path corrections* used in routine magnitude computation is one of the more useful contributions, since the underlying conventions are largely invisible in the primary literature.

**Focal-mechanism determination.** First-motion polarity-based focal-mechanism algorithms (FPFIT and predecessors, P-wave polarity inversion on the focal sphere) and moment-tensor inversion algorithms in their early-1980s form. The documentation includes the conventions for the focal-sphere projection, the assignment of nodal planes, and the statistical-confidence quantification of the mechanism solution.

A typical contribution in the volume runs ten to thirty pages: a short prose introduction, the FORTRAN source listing with comment annotations, an input-format specification, sample input and output, and a reference list pointing to the underlying primary papers. The compilation is consistent in format across contributions, which is itself an editorial achievement.

## Significance and limitations

The book's value is overwhelmingly *archival* rather than *didactic*. As of 2024 the practising seismologist learns the algorithms from modern textbooks (Shearer 2019 *Introduction to Seismology*; Stein & Wysession 2003 *An Introduction to Seismology*; Lay & Wallace 1995 *Modern Global Seismology*) and runs them via modern open-source toolkits (ObsPy in Python; SeisComP, Antelope, or IASPEI Software Library in operational environments). Engdahl 1984 is consulted when the user needs the *historical* definition of an algorithm — what HYPO71 actually computed, what the 1980s mb and MS conventions actually were, how SEIS83 handled interface conditions — typically because a re-processing or harmonisation effort requires the original definitions rather than their modern reimplementations. For the working exploration geophysicist, the volume's most-cited contribution is the SEIS83 documentation, which is the operational complement to Červený (2001).

Three limitations should be noted. First, the volume is *not a textbook*: a reader new to earthquake algorithms will find the contributions terse, format-heavy, and assuming the underlying primary papers. The right textbook companions are Lee & Stewart (1981) *Principles and Applications of Microearthquake Networks* and the IASPEI *New Manual of Seismological Observatory Practice* (Bormann 2012). Second, the algorithms documented are those of *the early 1980s*: subsequent developments — IASP91 and ak135 reference earth models, modern Bayesian and probabilistic location codes (NonLinLoc, Lomax et al. 2000; BayesLoc, Myers et al. 2007), waveform-based moment-tensor inversion (CMT, Dziewoński et al. 1981, but in its modern automated form), and double-difference relative-relocation methods (HypoDD, Waldhauser & Ellsworth 2000) — are absent because they post-date the volume. Third, the publication channel — a NOAA/USGS technical report rather than a primary monograph — gives it limited library distribution; many institutions hold the volume only on microfiche or as a scanned PDF, and direct citation often goes through a more accessible secondary reference (e.g., Červený 2001 for SEIS83; Lee & Lahr 1975 for HYPO71). The volume is most usefully thought of as the 1984 freeze-frame of observatory seismology's operational toolkit.

For our project the book is the natural reference whenever the question is *what was the operational definition of algorithm X in the 1980s*, in particular: the Geiger-style earthquake-location algorithms that underlie all modern microseismic-event-location codes (relevant to hydraulic-fracture monitoring and induced-seismicity studies); the spherical-earth ray-tracing and traveltime routines that are the lineage of the modern IASP91/ak135 traveltime tools; and — most directly — the SEIS83 documentation that is the operational complement to Červený (2001) for our ray-based forward-modelling work. It pairs naturally with Aki & Richards (1980) for the physical foundations of the algorithms, with Červený (2001) for the modern ray-theory framework, and with Bormann (2012) for the modern observatory-practice manual.
