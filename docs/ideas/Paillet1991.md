# Review: Paillet & Cheng (1991), Acoustic Waves in Boreholes

## Context and contribution

When it appeared in 1991, the Paillet–Cheng monograph filled a real gap: it was the first self-contained, book-length treatment of acoustic *waveform* logging, as opposed to the older first-arrival travel-time logging that had dominated petroleum geophysics for decades.The book provides a thorough review of the theory and interpretation techniques behind these applications, emphasizing the role of guided modes and critically refracted waves in shaping the recorded waveforms. Frederick Paillet (then at the U.S. Geological Survey) brought a hydrogeology and fractured-rock perspective; Chuen Hon (Arthur) Cheng came from MIT's Earth Resources Laboratory and the Full Waveform Acoustic Logging Consortium. The combination is a defining feature of the book — it consciously broadens the audience beyond reservoir engineers to include hydrogeologists, civil engineers, and crystalline-rock geophysicists.

## Structure and main ideas

The book follows a deliberate progression of increasing physical complexity, with each chapter pairing forward theory (synthetic microseismogram computation) with inversion and field case histories.

**1. Seismic properties of rocks.** The opening establishes the rock-physics background — elastic moduli, velocity–porosity relations, anisotropy parameters, and attenuation mechanisms — that any waveform interpretation must rely on.

**2. Axisymmetric waves in fluid-filled boreholes (isotropic case).** This is the conceptual core. Paillet and Cheng treat the classical monopole problem: a point pressure source on the axis of a fluid cylinder embedded in an elastic full-space. The recorded waveform is decomposed into critically refracted *head waves* (the P and S arrivals) and *guided modes* trapped in the fluid column. A central theme is that the seemingly complicated waveform is a superposition of a finite set of discrete normal modes and a continuum branch — and that nearly every interpretation problem reduces to identifying which mode carries the diagnostic information.A source pulse breaks up into a number of discrete mode peaks with successively increasing frequencies, generating complicated interference patterns.

**3. The Stoneley (tube) wave.** The fundamental m = 0 mode receives extended treatment because of its dual sensitivity: at low frequency its phase velocity gives shear slowness in slow formations (where the formation S-wave is slower than the borehole fluid and no S head wave exists), and its amplitude is attenuated by fluid flow into permeable or fractured walls.The radial component of the Stoneley wave forces fluid into fractures, which results in a loss of energy. The book is one of the more careful early treatments of the competing effects — true permeability-driven attenuation versus geometric attenuation from washouts, lithologic boundaries, or mudcake — that complicate the inversion.

**4. Pseudo-Rayleigh and higher-order modes.** These are the dispersive trapped modes that exist only above cutoff frequencies and only in fast formations. The authors show how the Airy phase and group-velocity minima leave characteristic signatures on the recorded waveform.

**5. Nonsymmetric (multipole) sources.** A substantial portion is devoted to dipole and quadrupole logging, which had become operationally important in the late 1980s. The motivation is shear-wave logging in slow formations: a dipole flexural mode propagates at the formation shear speed at low frequency even when no critically refracted S-wave exists, and the book develops both the mode theory and the dispersion-correction problem that the inversion requires.

**6. Transversely isotropic (TI) formations.** The treatment of intrinsic anisotropy — both vertical (VTI, e.g., shales) and horizontal symmetry axes — is one of the more advanced sections, and it links acoustic logging to the broader anisotropy literature that was expanding rapidly at the time.

**7. Porous, permeable formations.** Building on Biot's poroelastic framework and Rosenbaum's 1974 numerical implementation, the chapter shows how slow-wave coupling at the borehole wall produces Stoneley attenuation that scales with permeability, and discusses the conditions under which this attenuation is, or is not, practically detectable.

**8. Cased boreholes.** The final main topic addresses cement bond evaluation and the effect of steel casing and the casing–cement and cement–formation interfaces on the recorded modes — important both for production logging and for retro-fitting waveform analysis to older cased wells.

Throughout, **synthetic microseismograms** computed by discrete-wavenumber and real-axis integration methods are used as the bridge between theory and field data, and the appendices give the dispersion relations and integral representations in a single consistent notation.Each chapter includes the theory of synthetic microseismogram computation, interpretation and data inversion techniques illustrated using computed seismograms, and case histories using experimental data.

## Significance and limitations

The book's lasting strength is its insistence that *mode theory*, not ray theory, is the right organizing framework for borehole acoustics — and its physical, picture-first style makes that framework accessible to readers without a heavy applied-mathematics background. Its main dated aspects, recognized even by Paillet shortly afterward, are the limited treatment of cross-dipole anisotropy logging and of fracture-specific tube-wave responses, both of which matured in the 1990s. For those topics most readers now pair Paillet & Cheng with Tang and Cheng's *Quantitative Borehole Acoustic Methods* (Elsevier, 2004), but as a first principles introduction to *why* a borehole waveform looks the way it does, the 1991 book remains a standard reference and was reissued by Routledge/CRC in 2020 (ISBN 978-0367580001) and in a 2023 digital edition.
