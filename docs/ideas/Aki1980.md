# Review: Aki & Richards (1980), Quantitative Seismology: Theory and Methods

## Context and contribution

*Quantitative Seismology: Theory and Methods* by Keiiti Aki (then at MIT, later at USC) and Paul G. Richards (Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory of Columbia University) was published by W. H. Freeman in 1980 as a two-volume set running roughly 932 pages. It became, almost immediately on appearance, *the* standard graduate textbook of theoretical seismology, a position it retained essentially unchallenged until the authors' own second edition (University Science Books, 2002) two decades later. A generation of seismologists — academic, observatory and exploration — learned the elastodynamic representation theorem, the moment tensor, ray theory, mode summation and earthquake-source kinematics from this book, and the standard citation in any technical seismology paper to "the textbook treatment of [...]" defaults to Aki & Richards.

The book's distinctive contribution was to consolidate the post-1960 developments in seismology — the representation theorem in its modern form (Maruyama, Burridge–Knopoff), the seismic moment tensor (Gilbert, Backus), high-frequency ray theory and asymptotic expansion in vertically heterogeneous media (Červený, Chapman), normal-mode theory and free oscillations (Gilbert, Dziewoński), earthquake-source kinematics (Haskell, Brune, Madariaga), and attenuation and scattering (Knopoff, Aki) — into a single unified treatment with consistent notation. Before Aki & Richards, this material was scattered across roughly twenty primary papers and several review articles; after, it was a single self-contained reference whose Chapter 3 (representation of seismic sources) and Chapter 4 (elastic waves from a point dislocation source) became the citation for essentially all of moment-tensor seismology.

A second contribution, equally important for the working seismologist, is the book's *mathematical infrastructure*: a careful, self-contained derivation of the elastodynamic Green's tensor, of the representation theorem in its surface and volume forms, of the WKBJ asymptotic expansion for body waves and surface waves in vertically heterogeneous media, and of the normal-mode decomposition of the equations of motion for a stratified self-gravitating sphere. These derivations, given in textbook form for the first time, supplied the formal foundation on which both observatory-style global seismology and exploration-style reflection seismology subsequently built.

The book pre-dated the routine use of full-waveform inversion in exploration seismology, the development of seismic interferometry and ambient-noise tomography, and the modern reception of attenuation as a frequency-dependent rock-physics observable, but its formal apparatus is general enough that all three subsequent developments fit naturally inside the framework it sets out. The 2002 second edition adds an extra chapter on these post-1980 developments but largely preserves the original organization.

## Main ideas and structural progression

**Chapter 1 — Introduction.** A short orientation chapter that sets the central distinction between *kinematic* and *dynamic* seismology, between *forward* and *inverse* problems, and between *global / earthquake* seismology and *exploration / reflection* seismology, while arguing that the same elastodynamic mathematics underlies all four. The chapter announces the book's organization into a *theory* half (Chs. 2–9) and a *methods / applications* half (Chs. 10–15).

**Chapter 2 — Basic theorems in dynamic elasticity.** The mathematical foundation. The elastic equations of motion are derived from Cauchy's stress principle and Hooke's law; uniqueness theorems for the elastodynamic initial-boundary-value problem are stated and proved; the *elastodynamic representation theorem* — the statement that the displacement anywhere in an elastic body is the sum of contributions from body forces, surface tractions, and surface displacements weighted by the appropriate Green's tensor — is derived in volume and surface form and emerges as the central tool used in every subsequent chapter. The chapter's treatment of *reciprocity* (Betti's theorem) and of the *Green's tensor for an unbounded homogeneous medium* sets the conventions used throughout.

**Chapter 3 — Representation of seismic sources.** Probably the single most-cited chapter in the book. An indigenous fault dislocation is shown to be equivalent, at distances large compared with its size, to a system of equivalent body forces — specifically, to a *symmetric moment tensor* M whose components encode both the orientation and the amplitude of the dislocation. The double-couple representation of a shear dislocation is derived, the seismic moment Mij = μ A̅ ū dij is given its standard definition, and the radiation pattern of P, SV and SH waves from a point double couple is computed. This chapter is the citation behind essentially every moment-tensor inversion subsequently performed in earthquake seismology, and behind every exploration-seismic source-mechanism analysis (microseismic, hydraulic-fracture monitoring).

**Chapter 4 — Elastic waves from a point dislocation source.** The previous chapter's static result is extended to the time-dependent radiation from a propagating rupture. The chapter develops the far-field and near-field displacement fields explicitly, separates the geometrical (1/r) and static (1/r²) terms, and gives the body-wave and surface-wave radiation patterns. The Haskell rupture model — a one-dimensional rupture front propagating along a fault with a finite slip-rate function — is introduced and its directivity effect on the radiated displacement spectrum is derived.

**Chapter 5 — Plane waves in homogeneous media and their reflection and transmission at a plane boundary.** Plane P, SV and SH waves and their reflection and transmission at an interface between two homogeneous isotropic elastic half-spaces. The full Zoeppritz equations are derived in their standard form, the implicit Knott formulation is reconciled with the explicit matrix Zoeppritz form, and the post-critical (head-wave) regime is analysed via the complex angle of incidence. The treatment of *inhomogeneous plane waves* and of the *Stoneley wave* at a solid–solid interface is the canonical reference. The linearized Aki–Richards AVO approximation (the "Aki-Richards equation") appears in this chapter as the small-contrast, small-angle limit of the full Zoeppritz solution; it is the equation that, after Shuey's 1985 simplification, became the workhorse of exploration-seismic amplitude analysis.

**Chapter 6 — Reflection and refraction of spherical waves; Lamb's problem.** Beyond the plane-wave idealization, the chapter develops the full spherical-wave reflection-and-transmission problem at a planar interface, with the Cagniard–de Hoop technique for the time-domain solution, and *Lamb's problem* (the response of an elastic half-space to a surface point source) as the canonical worked example. The head-wave / refracted-wave analysis underlying refraction seismology and the long-offset behaviour of reflection seismograms is given here.

**Chapter 7 — Surface waves in a vertically heterogeneous medium.** Rayleigh and Love waves in a stack of horizontal layers, derived first by the Thomson–Haskell propagator-matrix method and then by the eigenfunction / normal-mode approach. The chapter develops the dispersion relations for the fundamental and higher modes, the group- and phase-velocity construction, and the energy-integral / variational expressions that connect mode eigenfunctions to dispersion measurements. The methodology underlies surface-wave tomography of the crust and lithosphere and, more recently, ambient-noise tomography (which postdates the 1980 edition).

**Chapter 8 — Free oscillations of the earth.** The eigenfrequency problem for the whole earth: spheroidal and toroidal modes, the SNREI (spherically symmetric, non-rotating, elastic, isotropic) reference earth, and the eigenfunction expansions used in long-period synthetic seismograms. The chapter is the textbook companion to the global normal-mode catalogues of Gilbert, Dziewoński, and Masters that emerged in the 1970s.

**Chapter 9 — Body waves in media with depth-dependent properties.** Ray theory and its asymptotic justification for vertically heterogeneous media. The chapter develops the ray equations and ray-parameter formalism, computes traveltime, slowness and amplitude along a ray, and treats turning points, caustics and shadow zones. The WKBJ approximation is derived as the high-frequency asymptotic of the wave equation, and the connection between geometric-optics rays and the wave equation is made precise. This is the chapter that exploration seismologists cite for *NMO theory*, *traveltime inversion*, and the *ray-Born* approximations behind early prestack migration.

**Chapter 10 — The seismic source: kinematics.** The earthquake source treated as a propagating rupture: rupture velocity, slip distribution, source duration, source spectra (corner frequency, high-frequency falloff), and the Brune, Haskell and Madariaga source models. The chapter is the bridge between Chapter 3's point-source moment tensor and the finite-fault analyses that occupy modern earthquake seismology.

**Chapter 11 — The seismic source: dynamics.** From kinematics to the underlying fracture mechanics: stress drop, frictional models, energy balance, and the radiation efficiency of an earthquake. The chapter foreshadows the rate-and-state friction literature that has dominated the subject since.

**Chapter 12 — Principles of seismometry.** Instrumentation: the equations of motion of a seismometer, transfer functions, frequency response, deconvolution to ground motion, and the design considerations behind broadband observatory instruments. The chapter is the textbook reference for any seismic-data analyst computing instrument-corrected ground motion.

**Chapter 13 — Wave propagation in an inelastic medium.** The attenuative side of seismic propagation: the complex modulus, the quality factor Q and its frequency dependence, causality constraints (Kramers–Kronig) and their consequences for velocity dispersion. The chapter is the foundation for everything written subsequently about constant-Q modelling and viscoelastic forward modelling (cf. Carcione 2022 for the modern computational version).

**Chapter 14 — Seismic waves in scattering media.** The Aki coda model. The late part of a seismogram, beyond the direct P, S and surface-wave arrivals, is treated as the cumulative scatter of seismic energy by small-scale heterogeneity in the crust and mantle. The chapter develops single-scattering and multiple-scattering models and shows that coda decay rates yield a measurement of crustal attenuation (Qc) that is largely independent of source–receiver path.

**Chapter 15 — The seismic source spectrum: dimensional analysis, similarity, and scaling laws.** A short concluding chapter on the empirical scaling relationships between seismic moment, source dimension, stress drop, and corner frequency — what is now known as the "seismic moment–corner frequency–stress drop similarity" — derived from dimensional analysis and the source models of Chapter 10.

## Significance and limitations

The book's enduring value is that it is the single reference where the full mathematical apparatus of theoretical seismology — elastodynamics, representation theorem, moment tensor, ray theory, mode summation, source kinematics and dynamics, attenuation, and scattering — is developed in one consistent notation and with one consistent level of mathematical rigour. Subsequent textbooks on specific subtopics (Dahlen & Tromp 1998 *Theoretical Global Seismology*; Shearer 2019 *Introduction to Seismology*; Stein & Wysession 2003 *An Introduction to Seismology, Earthquakes, and Earth Structure*; Červený 2001 *Seismic Ray Theory*) are each more thorough on a particular sub-domain but each cite Aki & Richards as the foundational reference. The Aki–Richards linearized AVO equation, the Aki coda model, the moment-tensor representation, and the WKBJ ray asymptotics each carry the authors' names because of this book.

Three limitations are worth flagging for the contemporary reader. First, the book is fundamentally *theoretical*: the *methods* half (Chs. 10–15) treats source physics, instrumentation and scattering in their classical 1970s form, and is now substantially out of date relative to modern instrument networks, ambient-noise interferometry, distributed-acoustic-sensing acquisition, and high-resolution earthquake-source inversion. Second, the treatment is essentially *isotropic and elastic*: anisotropic seismology (VTI, HTI, orthorhombic) appears only briefly, attenuation anisotropy is absent, and porous-medium effects (Biot's theory) are absent. For these the companion references are Tsvankin & Grechka (2011) on the anisotropic side and Carcione (2022) on the attenuation and porous-medium side. Third, the inverse-problem material — earthquake-source inversion, tomography, full-waveform inversion — is sketched only, with the formal inverse-theory machinery deferred to Tarantola (1987 / 2005); modern FWI literature treats the seismic inversion problem in a fundamentally different way from Aki & Richards' chapter on the topic. None of these qualifies the central claim that this is the foundational textbook of modern theoretical seismology.

For our project the book is the natural reference whenever the question involves the *theoretical foundations* of seismic wave propagation in isotropic elastic media: Chapter 2 for the elastodynamic Green's tensor and representation theorem (which is the formal basis of the seismic forward problem on which every inversion is built), Chapter 3 for the moment-tensor representation of point sources (microseismic / hydraulic-fracture monitoring), Chapter 5 for the Zoeppritz equations and the linearized Aki–Richards AVO formula (every AVO inversion), Chapter 9 for the WKBJ ray asymptotics (traveltime inversion, prestack migration), and Chapter 13 for the causality / Kramers–Kronig constraints on attenuative wave propagation (Q-FWI consistency tests). It pairs naturally with Carcione (2022) for the anisotropic-attenuating-porous extensions, with Tsvankin & Grechka (2011) for the anisotropic-AVO extension, and with Tarantola (2005) for the inverse-problem machinery the methods half of the book leaves under-developed.

Brune, J. N. (1970). Tectonic stress and the spectra of seismic shear waves from earthquakes. *Journal of Geophysical Research*, 75, 4997–5009. (Source spectrum and corner-frequency model of Chs. 10 and 15.)

Madariaga, R. (1976). Dynamics of an expanding circular fault. *Bulletin of the Seismological Society of America*, 66, 639–666. (Dynamic-rupture model of Ch. 11.)

Shuey, R. T. (1985). A simplification of the Zoeppritz equations. *Geophysics*, 50, 609–614. (Shuey's two-term approximation of the Aki–Richards linearization of Ch. 5; the practical AVO equation used in exploration.)

Dahlen, F. A., & Tromp, J. (1998). *Theoretical Global Seismology.* Princeton University Press. (Modern global-seismology successor; more thorough on mode and tomographic theory than Chs. 8 and 9.)

Shearer, P. M. (2019). *Introduction to Seismology* (3rd ed.). Cambridge University Press. (Undergraduate / first-graduate textbook companion.)

Červený, V. (2001). *Seismic Ray Theory.* Cambridge University Press. (Modern, dedicated treatment of the ray asymptotics of Ch. 9.)

Tarantola, A. (2005). *Inverse Problem Theory and Methods for Model Parameter Estimation.* SIAM. (Inverse-problem framework that underlies the methods half of Aki & Richards in its modern form.)

Carcione, J. M. (2022). *Wave Fields in Real Media* (4th ed.). Amsterdam: Elsevier. (Anisotropic, attenuating and porous-medium extensions absent from Aki & Richards.)

Tsvankin, I., & Grechka, V. (2011). *Seismology of Azimuthally Anisotropic Media and Seismic Fracture Characterization.* Tulsa, OK: SEG. (Anisotropic-AVO extension absent from Aki & Richards.)
